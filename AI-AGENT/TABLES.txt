1. Our table naming convention
I recommend using functional prefixes in addition to the common suffix.

For example:
- biz_ – Business management
- auth_ – Authentication and users
- cat_ – Product categories
- prod_ – Products
- cust_ – Customers
- sales_ – Sales and orders
- vend_ – Vendors
- purch_ – Purchases
- inv_ – Inventory
- fin_ – Finance and expenses
- agent_ – AI agent
- audit_ – Audit and activity logs
This makes related tables immediately identifiable.

Important relationships explained

Relationship	Type	Explanation
Business → Users	One-to-many	One business can have multiple users
Category → Products	One-to-many	One category can contain many products
Customer → Orders	One-to-many	One customer can place multiple orders
Order → Order Items	One-to-many	One order can contain multiple products
Product → Order Items	One-to-many	One product can appear in multiple orders
Vendor → Purchase Orders	One-to-many	One vendor can supply multiple purchases
Product → Inventory	One-to-one	One current inventory record per product
Product → Stock Movements	One-to-many	One product can have many stock transactions

6. Important database constraints and indexing
We should not just create tables. We must also ensure the relationships remain consistent.

These are the constraints I recommend.
Table	Important constraints
Businesses	Unique business code
Users	Unique email within a business
Roles	Unique role name within a business
Categories	Unique category name within a business
Products	Unique SKU within a business
Customers	Unique customer email where supplied
Orders	Unique order number within a business
Inventory	One inventory record per product per business
Purchase Orders	Unique purchase number within a business
Order Items	Quantity greater than zero
Expenses	Amount greater than zero

We will use:
- ON DELETE RESTRICT for important business records.
- ON DELETE CASCADE for dependent chat messages when a chat session is intentionally deleted, subject to our retention policy.
- ON DELETE SET NULL only for optional relationships where preserving history makes sense.

8. Example business analytical SQL queries
Before connecting Gemini, we should verify that our backend can answer business questions using SQL.

Example 1 – Total monthly revenue


SELECT
    DATE_TRUNC('month', order_date) AS month,
    SUM(total_amount) AS revenue
FROM sales_orders_biz_agent
WHERE business_id = $1
  AND order_status = 'COMPLETED'
GROUP BY DATE_TRUNC('month', order_date)
ORDER BY month;

This is a starting query. Our final revenue service will also apply the agreed refund, discount and tax rules.

Example 2 – Top five products

SELECT
    p.product_name,
    SUM(oi.quantity) AS units_sold,
    SUM(oi.line_total) AS total_revenue
FROM sales_order_items_biz_agent oi
JOIN sales_orders_biz_agent o
    ON o.id = oi.order_id
JOIN prod_products_biz_agent p
    ON p.id = oi.product_id
WHERE o.business_id = $1
  AND o.order_status = 'COMPLETED'
GROUP BY p.id, p.product_name
ORDER BY total_revenue DESC
LIMIT 5;

Example 3 – Products below reorder level
SELECT
    p.product_name,
    i.stock_quantity,
    p.reorder_level
FROM inv_inventory_biz_agent i
JOIN prod_products_biz_agent p
    ON p.id = i.product_id
WHERE i.business_id = $1
  AND i.stock_quantity <= p.reorder_level
ORDER BY i.stock_quantity;


Example 4 – Monthly expense summary
SELECT
    DATE_TRUNC('month', expense_date) AS month,
    expense_category,
    SUM(amount) AS total_expense
FROM fin_expenses_biz_agent
WHERE business_id = $1
GROUP BY
    DATE_TRUNC('month', expense_date),
    expense_category
ORDER BY month, total_expense DESC;


Notice that all these queries use a parameter for business_id. This will be supplied by our authenticated backend context, not by the AI model.
